Stay, switch, or move? A Kansas farmer's climate decision

Summers are getting hotter, droughts are coming more often, and crop insurance keeps getting more expensive. This page lets you test three choices against different futures and see which one keeps the farm profitable.

The issue

Our decision maker farms about 780 acres (the average Kansas farm) and grows dryland winter wheat. Climate change is reaching the farm three ways:

  • Heat. Wheat and corn yields fall steeply once temperatures pass critical thresholds (about 29–30 °C / 84–86 °F during key growth stages). Each extra degree costs more than the one before.
  • Drought. Kansas had serious droughts in 2011–14, 2018 and 2022–23. In 2023 the state's wheat harvest was its smallest since the 1950s. Hotter air dries soils faster, so every drought hurts more.
  • Insurance. Crop-insurance premiums rise as losses pile up and as risk is re-priced, which cuts into already thin margins.

The farmer is weighing three options:

Stay: keep wheat

Change nothing. Wheat is familiar and the equipment is paid for, but wheat is sensitive to heat and drought.

Switch: grain sorghum

Stay in Kansas but plant sorghum, which handles heat and drought better. It has lower prices and needs some new equipment.

Move: Texas Blackland Prairie

Sell up and buy deep, fertile clay soils with more rain (about 38 in. a year) and grow corn. But Texas is already hotter, and the land costs more.

What each slider represents

Extra warming (hazard). How much hotter the growing season becomes. The yield damage is non-linear: going from +3 to +4 °C does much more harm than going from 0 to +1 °C. Places that are already hot (Central Texas) cross the damage threshold sooner.

Drought frequency (hazard). How many years in ten have a damaging drought. In a drought year wheat loses about 35% of its yield, Texas corn on deep clay soils about 28%, and sorghum about 22%. Warming makes each drought worse.

Irrigated share (vulnerability). How exposed the Kansas farm is. Irrigated acres lose about 85% less in a drought and are cheaper to insure, but irrigation costs money every year and that cost rises with heat. This slider decides how much the drought slider matters.

Insurance premium increase (cost). The rising premiums the farmer is already seeing. Revenue-protection insurance pays back part of drought losses, but the farmer pays a premium every year.

Texas land price (cost). Moving means selling Kansas land and buying Texas land. Any price gap is borrowed, and the interest, plus moving costs spread over 20 years, counts against the Texas option.

What to notice as you move the sliders

  1. Drought only hurts as much as the farm is exposed. With no irrigation, moving drought from 1 to 6 years per decade cuts wheat's return by about $47/acre. With half the farm irrigated, the same move costs only about $31. The vulnerability slider changes how much the hazard slider matters. Irrigation isn't free, though: in a wet future it lowers profit, and it only pays off once droughts are frequent. Its cost also rises as the climate warms.
  2. Moving south doesn't escape the heat. The Texas line starts high, because fertile soil and more rain give big corn yields, but it falls fastest as warming rises. Texas corn already grows close to its heat limit, so each added degree hurts it more than it hurts Kansas crops.
  3. Sorghum is the hedge. Sorghum earns less than wheat in a mild climate. But its line slopes down more gently, so it overtakes wheat as heat and drought build. On the decision map, sorghum covers the hot, dry corner.
  4. Insurance costs pile on top of the hazards. Premiums rise with the slider and with drought risk, so a high premium increase in a drought-prone future can push a thin wheat margin below zero.
  5. No option wins everywhere. The decision map shows which choice is most robust across futures. That matters because the farmer can't know which future will arrive.

How the model works

For each option the page calculates an average-year net return per acre:

yield = base yield × (1 − heat loss) × (1 − drought loss)
heat loss = sensitivity × ΔT × (1 + 0.15 × (ΔT + how hot the place already is)) × (1 − 0.35 × irrigated share)
drought loss = (droughts per decade ÷ 10) × drought-year loss × (1 − 0.85 × irrigated share) × (1 + 0.12 × ΔT)
net = yield × price + insurance payouts − production cost − irrigation cost − premium − switching / moving cost

Insurance payouts are set at 40% of drought losses. Premiums scale with the premium slider, with drought frequency, and with warming. One-time costs (new sorghum equipment, about $50/acre; moving, about $150,000) are spread over 20 years. For Texas, the gap between the Texas land price and the after-cost Kansas sale price is financed at 6.5%. Whole-farm figures assume 780 acres for every option.

Limits of this model

This is a teaching model, not farm advice. It uses average years, so it hides the cash-flow shock of back-to-back droughts. It ignores price changes caused by regional crop failures, government disaster aid, and family and community ties that make moving costly in ways money doesn't capture. It also ignores the long-run decline of the Ogallala aquifer, which may make Kansas irrigation unavailable at any price.
