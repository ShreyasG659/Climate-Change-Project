Wheat or sorghum? A Kansas farmer's climate decision

Summers are getting hotter, droughts are coming more often, and crop insurance keeps getting more expensive. Should this farm keep growing wheat or switch to a crop built for heat? Move the sliders to find the tipping point.

The issue

Our decision maker farms about 780 acres (the average Kansas farm) and grows winter wheat. Climate change is reaching the farm three ways:

  • Heat. Wheat yields fall steeply once temperatures pass critical thresholds (about 30 °C / 86 °F while the grain is filling). Each extra degree costs more than the one before.
  • Drought. Kansas had serious droughts in 2011–14, 2018 and 2022–23. In 2023 the state's wheat harvest was its smallest since the 1950s. Hotter air dries soils faster, so every drought hurts more.
  • Insurance. Crop-insurance premiums rise as losses pile up and as risk is re-priced, which cuts into already thin margins.

The farmer has two options:

Keep winter wheat

Change nothing. Wheat is familiar, the equipment is paid for, and it earns more per bushel. But wheat is sensitive to heat and drought.

Switch to grain sorghum

Sorghum originated in hot, dry Africa and handles heat and drought much better. It sells for less per bushel, costs more to grow, and needs some new equipment.

Set the scenario

Growing-season warming above today's climate, in °C. About +1 °C by the 2040s; +2 to 3 °C by late century under high emissions.

Years per decade with a damaging drought. Kansas has averaged about 3 recently.

More irrigation means less exposure to drought, but pumping costs rise as it gets hotter. The Ogallala aquifer limits this in western Kansas.

Rise in farmer-paid crop-insurance premiums over today's rates. Premiums also climb automatically as droughts and heat get worse.

What each slider represents

Extra warming (hazard). How much hotter the growing season becomes. The yield damage is non-linear: going from +3 to +4 °C does much more harm than going from 0 to +1 °C. Wheat loses about 6% of its yield per °C, compared with about 3.5% for sorghum.

Drought frequency (hazard). How many years in ten have a damaging drought. In a drought year dryland wheat loses about 38% of its yield and sorghum about 20%. Warming makes each drought worse.

Irrigated share (vulnerability). How exposed the farm is to drought. Irrigated acres lose about 85% less in a drought and are cheaper to insure. But irrigation costs money every year, and that cost rises with heat. This slider decides how much the two hazard sliders matter, and it shifts the tipping point between the crops.

Insurance premium increase (cost). The rising premiums the farmer is already seeing. Revenue-protection insurance pays back part of drought losses, but the farmer pays a premium every year.

What to notice as you move the sliders

  1. Wheat wins in a mild climate; sorghum wins in a hot one. With little warming and few droughts, wheat earns about $15 an acre more. Its higher price outweighs sorghum's bigger yields. Wheat's line falls faster as warming rises, though, and the two lines cross at the tipping point shown above the chart.
  2. The tipping point depends on vulnerability. With no irrigation, sorghum pulls ahead after only about +0.6 °C of warming. With 30% of the farm irrigated, the tipping point moves to about +2 °C. At 60% it is about +3.4 °C. Irrigation shields wheat from drought, so the farm can wait longer before switching.
  3. The same drought means different things on different farms. On an all-dryland farm, going from 1 to 6 droughts per decade costs wheat about $50 an acre and flips the decision to sorghum. With half the farm irrigated, the same drought costs wheat about $34, and wheat stays ahead.
  4. Insurance premiums squeeze both crops, but they don't pick the winner. Raising premiums from +0% to +150% lowers both lines by about the same amount, because both crops pay similar premiums. Premiums decide whether farming stays profitable, not which crop to grow.
  5. Watch the faded cells on the decision map. In the hot, dry corner sorghum is the better choice but still loses money. Switching crops slows the damage; it doesn't stop it.

How the model works

For each crop the page calculates an average-year net return per acre:

yield = base yield × (1 − heat loss) × (1 − drought loss)
heat loss = heat sensitivity × ΔT × (1 + 0.15 × (ΔT + 1)) × (1 − 0.35 × irrigated share)
drought loss = (droughts per decade ÷ 10) × drought-year loss × (1 − 0.85 × irrigated share) × (1 + 0.12 × ΔT)
net = yield × price + insurance payouts − production cost − irrigation cost − premium − switching cost

Base yield blends dryland and irrigated yields by the irrigated share. Insurance payouts are set at 40% of drought losses. Premiums scale with the premium slider, with drought exposure, and with warming. Switching to sorghum has a one-time cost of about $50/acre (row-crop planter, header, learning curve), spread over 20 years. Whole-farm figures multiply by 780 acres.

Limits of this model

This is a teaching model, not farm advice. It uses average years, so it hides the cash-flow shock of back-to-back droughts. It ignores price changes caused by regional crop failures, government disaster aid, crop rotations that mix both crops, and the long-run decline of the Ogallala aquifer, which may make irrigation unavailable at any price.
